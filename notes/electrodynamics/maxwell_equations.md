# Maxwell Equations

### Maxwell Equations in Vacuum

Let $\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)$ and $\mathbf B(\mathbf r,t)$ be the electric and magnetic fields. Let $\rho(\mathbf r,t)$ and $\mathbf j(\mathbf r,t)$ be the charge and current densities. The constants $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are the vacuum permittivity and permeability.

1. Gauss's law relates the divergence of the electric field to the charge density:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}}.
   $$

2. Faraday's law states that a time-dependent magnetic field produces a circulating electric field:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}}.
   $$

3. Gauss's law for magnetism states that the magnetic field has no sources or sinks:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0}.
   $$

4. The Ampère–Maxwell law states that electric current and a time-dependent electric field produce a circulating magnetic field:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}}.
   $$

**Charge Conservation**

Taking the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell equation and using Gauss's law gives

$$
0=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf j+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)=\mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf j+\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right).
$$

Therefore, the charge and current densities satisfy the continuity equation

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0}.
$$

The displacement-current term in the Ampère–Maxwell equation ensures compatibility with charge conservation.

**Electromagnetic Potentials and Gauge Symmetry**

Let $\Phi(\mathbf r,t)$ be a differentiable scalar field and $\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)$ a differentiable vector field. The gradient and curl satisfy

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\Phi)=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=0.
$$

The magnetic field can be represented by the vector potential:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.
$$

Substitution into Faraday's law gives

$$
\nabla\times\left(\mathbf E+\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t}\right)=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf E+\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t}=-\nabla\Phi.
$$

The electromagnetic fields are therefore represented by

$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t},\qquad \mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A}.
$$

Let $\chi(\mathbf r,t)$ be an arbitrary differentiable scalar function. The transformation

$$
\boxed{\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\qquad \Phi'=\Phi-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}}
$$

is a gauge symmetry. The transformed potentials generate the same electromagnetic fields:

$$
\mathbf B'=\nabla\times\mathbf A'=\mathbf B,\qquad \mathbf E'=-\nabla\Phi'-\frac{\partial\mathbf A'}{\partial t}=\mathbf E.
$$

Thus, the electromagnetic potentials are not unique, while the physical fields are gauge invariant.

### Maxwell Equations in Matter

**Polarization and Magnetization**

Let $\mathbf P$ be the electric dipole moment per unit volume and $\mathbf M$ the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. Let $\rho_b$ and $\mathbf j_b$ be the bound charge and current densities generated by the material response:

$$
\boxed{\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\qquad \mathbf j_b=\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t}+\nabla\times\mathbf M}.
$$

Let $\rho_f$ and $\mathbf j_f$ be the free charge and current densities. The total sources are

$$
\boxed{\rho=\rho_f+\rho_b,\qquad \mathbf j=\mathbf j_f+\mathbf j_b}.
$$

Define the electric displacement field $\mathbf D$ and magnetic field intensity $\mathbf H$ by

$$
\boxed{\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P,\qquad \mathbf H=\frac{\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\mathbf M}.
$$

With these definitions, the Maxwell equations in matter depend explicitly only on the free sources.

**Field Equations**

1. Gauss's law relates the divergence of the electric displacement field to the free charge density:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f}.
   $$

2. Faraday's law retains its vacuum form:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}}.
   $$

3. Gauss's law for magnetism retains its vacuum form:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0}.
   $$

4. The Ampère–Maxwell law relates the circulation of $\mathbf H$ to the free current and the time-dependent electric displacement:

   $$
   \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf j_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}}.
   $$

**Constitutive Relations**

Maxwell's equations are closed by material-dependent constitutive relations. For a linear, isotropic, nondispersive medium, let $\chi_e$ and $\chi_m$ be the electric and magnetic susceptibilities:

$$
\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad \mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H.
$$

Define the permittivity $\epsilon$ and permeability $\mu$ by

$$
\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e),\qquad \mu=\mu_0(1+\chi_m).
$$

For an Ohmic conductor, let $\sigma$ be the electrical conductivity. The constitutive relations are

$$
\boxed{\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad \mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H,\qquad \mathbf j_f=\sigma\mathbf E}.
$$

Anisotropic, nonlinear, and dispersive media require more general constitutive relations.

### Boundary Conditions

Consider a stationary interface between media $1$ and $2$. Let $\hat{\mathbf n}$ be the unit normal directed from medium $1$ to medium $2$. Let $\sigma_f$ be the free surface charge density and $\mathbf K_f$ the free surface current density tangent to the interface. Subscripts $1$ and $2$ denote the limiting field values on the two sides. Let $d\mathbf a$, $d\mathbf l$, and $dV$ denote oriented area, oriented line, and volume elements.

**Gauss's Law for Electric Displacement**

Let $V_h$ be a pillbox of height $h$ crossing the interface, with face area $\Delta S$. The divergence theorem gives

$$
\oint_{\partial V_h}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{V_h}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D)\,dV=\int_{V_h}\rho_f\,dV.
$$

As $h\to0$, the side flux vanishes and the free-charge integral approaches $\sigma_f\Delta S$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)\Delta S=\sigma_f\Delta S.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f}.
$$

**Gauss's Law for Magnetism**

For the same pillbox, the divergence theorem gives

$$
\oint_{\partial V_h}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{V_h}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)\,dV=0.
$$

As $h\to0$, the side flux vanishes:

$$
\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)\Delta S=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0}.
$$

**Faraday's Law**

Let $S_h$ be a narrow rectangular surface crossing the interface, with height $h$ and long-side length $\Delta l$. Let $\hat{\mathbf t}$ be the unit tangent along its long sides. Stokes' theorem gives

$$
\oint_{\partial S_h}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_{S_h}(\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf a=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{S_h}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a.
$$

As $h\to0$, the short-side contributions and magnetic flux vanish for finite $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$:

$$
(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}\,\Delta l=0.
$$

Because $\hat{\mathbf t}$ is arbitrary,

$$
\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=0}.
$$

**Ampère–Maxwell Law**

For the same rectangular surface, Stokes' theorem gives

$$
\oint_{\partial S_h}\mathbf H\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_{S_h}(\nabla\times\mathbf H)\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{S_h}\mathbf j_f\cdot d\mathbf a+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{S_h}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a.
$$

As $h\to0$, the short-side contributions and electric-displacement flux vanish for finite $\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf D$. The free surface current density $\mathbf K_f$ remains:

$$
(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}\,\Delta l=\mathbf K_f\cdot(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t})\,\Delta l.
$$

Because $\hat{\mathbf t}$ is arbitrary,

$$
\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f}.
$$

When $\sigma_f=0$ and $\mathbf K_f=\mathbf0$, the corresponding normal and tangential components are continuous across the interface.
